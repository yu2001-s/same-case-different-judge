• The assigned judge matters, measurably. In {{n_six_fmt}} single-judge drunk-driving judgments from six district courts, judges assigned to the same pool of cases sentenced systematically differently after controlling for breath-alcohol level, prior record, crashes and vehicle. About {{icc}} of the variation that remains after case facts and court is tied to the judge (p {{lr_p}}).
  • The differences are stable, not noise. Splitting each judge's cases at random into two halves, a judge's leniency in one half predicts it in the other (correlation {{split_r}}). Shuffling judge labels within assignment pools never produced variation as large as the real data ({{n_perm}} shuffles, p {{perm_p}}).
  • For the same typical first offence, a lenient-decile judge and a harsh-decile judge differ by about {{gap_days}} days on a sentence that averages {{mean_sentence_mo}} months. Courts also differ: the same case is predicted to draw different sentences in different cities.
  • The widest discretion sits in the add-ons. Nationally, whether a judge adds a fine on top of the prison term ranges from about {{nat_fine_p10}} of cases for lenient-decile judges to {{nat_fine_p90}} for strict-decile judges. Probation is rare, and {{nat_prob_never}} of judges with 30+ cases never granted it.
  • Timing barely shows up. {{timing_sig}} of {{timing_n}} calendar factors (weekday, month-end, days before long holidays, heavy signing days, December) survive correction for multiple tests; the largest uncorrected difference is about {{timing_max}} days on a {{mean_sentence_days}}-day average sentence. Public judgments record only the date, so a true time-of-day (hungry-judge) test is not possible with these data.
  • The same pattern holds nationwide. In {{n_nat_fmt}} cases from all {{nat_courts}} district courts (official microdata, fewer controls), the judge accounts for {{nat_icc}} of the residual variation and judge rankings are again stable across random halves (r = {{nat_split}}).
